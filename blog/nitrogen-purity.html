Nitrogen Purity: 99.5% vs 99.9%

The purity spec decides two bills at once: the gas invoice and the polishing labor. The right number depends on what the customer sees on the edge.

Nitrogen assist gas is judged on purity because the remaining fraction is oxygen — and oxygen is exactly what nitrogen is there to exclude. On stainless, stray oxygen shows up as heat tint: the yellow-brown edge that means pickling or polishing before the part ships.

When 99.5% is enough

For parts that get welded, coated or machined after cutting, 99.5% nitrogen is usually the cheaper and correct choice. Thin stainless for internal brackets is a textbook case: nobody sees the tint, and membrane-generated nitrogen at 99.5% costs a fraction of high-purity supply.

When 99.9%+ pays for itself

Architectural stainless, food equipment and anything sold on appearance need the brighter edge. High-purity nitrogen — from cylinders, liquid dewars or PSA generators — removes most post-cut finishing. Shops that moved from 99.5% to 99.9% for visible parts commonly report the labor saved on polishing covers the extra gas cost.

Supply modes compared

  • Cylinders — simple, expensive per cubic meter, fine below a few hours of cutting per week.
  • Membrane generator — lowest cost at 95–99.5%; the workhorse for non-cosmetic work.
  • PSA generator — 99.9%+ on tap; the break-even usually lands somewhere near daily nitrogen use.
  • Liquid supply — for heavy continuous consumption, priced by contract.
