Focus Position and Nozzle Condition

Two settings ruin more good setups than power ever does: where the beam focuses, and what the nozzle has been through since breakfast.

When an edge suddenly develops dross or the striations turn rough, the parameter list is rarely the culprit. Focus drift and nozzle damage are. Both change the power density at the kerf without changing a single number on the screen.

Focus position

Thin sheet is usually cut with focus at or just below the surface; thick plate wants the focal point deeper in the section so the kerf stays wide enough to eject melt. When focus sits too shallow on thick plate, the bottom of the cut solidifies before the gas can clear it — the classic stubborn dross that grinding will not forgive.

Nozzle condition

A nozzle that has kissed a warped sheet once may look fine and cut wrong. The hole decides gas flow geometry: a burred or oval nozzle tip turns a laminar assist-gas jet into turbulence, and the edge tells on you within minutes. Centering matters too — an off-center jet effectively cuts with less gas on one side.

The ten-minute daily routine

  • Check nozzle bore with a centering gauge before the first cut, not after the first scrap.
  • Wipe the protective glass; a smeared lens shifts effective focus without warning.
  • Run a focus test cut on known material when the material type or thickness range changes.
  • Replace, do not file, damaged nozzles — they cost less than the parts they ruin.
